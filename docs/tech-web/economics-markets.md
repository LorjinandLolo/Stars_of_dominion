# Economics & Markets

Playing into this domain means treating credits, contracts, and confidence as weapons and territory. Where a military civilization projects fleets down hyperlanes, an economic civilization projects balance sheets: it owns the exchange where its rivals' ore is priced, holds the paper on its neighbours' colony programs, and clears the transactions that everyone else's trade depends on. Its instruments are the ones already living in the simulation — the `Faction.creditSupply`/`liquidity`/`debt` triple, theatre markets keyed `theatreId:resource`, charter corporations with their rights and autonomy drift, trade routes with piracy and blockade risk — and this domain turns each of them from bookkeeping into a lever a player can pull. The characteristic experience is indirect power: nothing in this tree fires a shot, but by Tier 3 a committed economic power can bankrupt a rival's war effort, seize collateral it lent against three seasons ago, and make neutrality unaffordable.

The civilization identity this enables is the corporate-mercantile state — and, crucially, more than one version of it. The Tier 2 fork decides whether capital serves the state or the state serves capital, which cascades through the whole charter-corporation layer (autonomy drift, demand frequency, foreign ownership). The Tier 4 fork asks the question the domain has been building toward: when molecular manufacturing and fusion abundance arrive, does the market become the government, or does the government finally abolish the market? Both endstates are irreversible civilizational statements, and both retire problems the player has spent the whole game managing — at the price of new ones no other civilization has.

## Tier 1 — Expansion

### Interstellar Banking (`econ_interstellar_banking`)
- **Domain:** economy | **Tier:** 1
- **Prerequisites:** comm_deep_space_network
- **Concept:** Credit across interplanetary distances requires settlement finality despite light-lag — banking becomes possible only once a relay network can carry signed ledger updates faster than a freighter can carry rumors. Interstellar banks are therefore communications institutions before they are financial ones.
- **What becomes possible:** Sovereign borrowing and lending as deliberate policy. The state can raise credits today against tomorrow's tax base, and can hold other governments' paper — becoming a creditor with a creditor's interests.
- **Gameplay mechanic unlocked:** Sovereign debt instruments. The faction may issue bonds (credits now, coupon drain per tick, principal recorded against `Faction.debt`) and extend loans to other factions or to charter corporations, creating persistent `LoanContract` records with negotiated coupon, term, and (later, with `econ_debt_leverage`) collateral. Coupon rates float on the borrower's `metrics.confidenceIndex` — an unstable regime pays more, which players can see and exploit. Lending to a faction gives you a standing stake in its survival: a new diplomatic posture the game currently has no way to express.
- **Engine hook:** New order types `ECON_ISSUE_BONDS` and `ECON_EXTEND_LOAN` handled in scripts/game-loop.ts alongside the `CORP_*` block; a `loanContracts: Map<string, LoanContract>` added to `EconomyWorldState`; coupon settlement runs in the economy tick against `Faction.liquidity`/`debt`. Unlock flag `banking_core`; new capital-world building `central_reserve_bank` (required capacity, below). This tech should replace `'eco_t1_2'` as `CHARTER_TECH_ID` in lib/economy/corporate/charter-service.ts, making banking the gate for founding charters at all.
- **Required capacity:** Knowledge alone permits nothing. Issuing or holding debt requires a `central_reserve_bank` built on a `commercial`-type planet with adequate `energyProduced` headroom, plus a minimum credits reserve (fractional backing — the bank cannot lend more than a multiple of its vault). Loan servicing runs over the deep-space relay network; if the borrower's route to your banking world is fully blockaded (`BlockadeState`), coupons stop arriving.
- **Interactions:** The charter-corp `banking` mission and `bank_branch` assets plug directly into this: chartered banks can originate loans on the state's behalf, taking a spread. Bond issuance interacts with the press system — a downgraded confidence story moves your coupon rate. Feeds the WEALTH season-score category.
- **Strategic advantages:** Smooths the lumpy early economy (borrow for the colony ship now), and opens the entire rest of this tree. Being the first creditor in a region means every later crisis is an opportunity.
- **Vulnerabilities & consequences:** Debt service is a permanent per-tick drain that competes with fleet upkeep; a faction that borrows into a war it loses enters a default spiral (confidence down → coupons up → more borrowing). Your loan book is an attack surface: debtors who repudiate cost you real credits, and espionage ops against your reserve bank (a new `ESP_*` target) can expose or falsify your books, spiking `inflationRate`.
- **Emergent follow-ons:** Creditor blocs defending debtor client states; deliberate over-lending as a prelude to `econ_debt_leverage`; parliaments voting down budgets and forcing bond issuance as the path of least political capital.

### Open Commodity Exchanges (`econ_commodity_exchanges`)
- **Domain:** economy | **Tier:** 1
- **Prerequisites:** econ_interstellar_banking, space_orbital_logistics
- **Concept:** Markets need standardization before they need speculation: an exchange exists when a "lot of metals" means the same graded, containerized quantity at every port. Standardized orbital cargo handling is what turns bilateral haggling into an order book.
- **What becomes possible:** Public, priced, anonymous spot trade. Instead of negotiating a `TradeAgreement` with a specific counterparty, a faction can simply sell into or buy from the exchange at the posted theatre price — and everyone can see that price.
- **Gameplay mechanic unlocked:** Exchange-hosted spot trading and the information economy around it. A faction that builds an exchange chooses one system as the venue; every trade there pays the host a fee, and the exchange publishes aggregate supply/demand per resource to all members — a legitimate intelligence feed that partially substitutes for espionage. Hosting is a strategic act: the venue system becomes economically load-bearing for every member, including your rivals.
- **Engine hook:** New orbital structure `commodity_exchange` (one per theatre); new order `ECON_SPOT_TRADE {resource, volume, side}` executing against the existing `Market` map (`theatreId:resource`, `currentPrice` from `updateMarketsAggregated`), with the fee credited to the host faction each economy tick. Unlock flag `resource_exchange_access`. Exchange membership list stored in `EconomyWorldState`; member supply/demand aggregates exposed through the `/api/game/sync` snapshot so the UI can chart them.
- **Required capacity:** The exchange must be built in a system the host controls that is already a `TradeHub` with `routeCount >= 3` — an exchange nobody's freight passes through is a building, not a market. Spot deliveries consume real haulage: trades settle through the logistics/distribution layer, so a planet with failing `PlanetLogisticsState` can buy on the exchange and still watch the goods rot in orbit.
- **Interactions:** Charter corps with the `trade` mission route their revenue through exchanges, raising both their income and the host's fee take. Exchange price feeds are what `econ_automated_markets` and `econ_futures_markets` trade against. Piracy against routes feeding the venue system visibly moves prices — a readable early-warning display.
- **Strategic advantages:** Fee income scales with everyone's trade, not just yours; the intelligence feed is free; and hosting confers agenda power later (`econ_market_warfare` raids are harder to run against the venue's own host).
- **Vulnerabilities & consequences:** Price transparency cuts both ways: your shortages are now public the moment you buy. The venue system becomes a chokepoint in the economic graph — blockading it (existing `BlockadeState` mechanics) freezes spot trade for the whole theatre, making your flagship market a military target. Local sellers on low-happiness worlds resent exports of scarce food at exchange prices: exporting a resource whose `commodityScarcity` flag is set at home raises `instability`.
- **Emergent follow-ons:** Rival exchanges in adjacent theatres competing on fees; wars fought to blockade a venue rather than take territory; the exchange feed becoming the de facto galactic newspaper of record, which the press system can quote or contradict.

### Joint-Stock Charters (`econ_public_charters`)
- **Domain:** economy | **Tier:** 1
- **Prerequisites:** econ_interstellar_banking
- **Concept:** The joint-stock company is a technology for aggregating strangers' capital: tradeable shares plus limited liability let a venture outlive and outgrow any single backer. Historically it is what turned trading posts into empires-within-empires.
- **What becomes possible:** Charter corporations stop being wholly state-held instruments and become publicly traded entities whose ownership — and therefore loyalty — is contested on an open market.
- **Gameplay mechanic unlocked:** Public listing of charters. The state may float a chartered corporation, selling equity for an immediate credits windfall and a share price that thereafter moves with the company's books. Once listed, the existing `CORP_BUY_SHARES` / `CORP_SELL_SHARES` / `CORP_HOSTILE_TAKEOVER` orders open to *foreign* factions: other players can quietly accumulate stakes in your companies, and you in theirs. Dividend policy becomes a per-company decision (retain for expansion vs pay out to the treasuries that hold it).
- **Engine hook:** New order `CORP_LIST_PUBLIC {companyId, floatFraction}` in the game-loop `CORP_*` block; extends the charter share model already exercised by `sharePriceMultiplier` effects in `CRISIS_DEFS` (accounting_fraud, shareholder_revolt, foreign_acquisition — all of which get sharper teeth once shares are actually tradeable cross-faction). Unlock flag `public_listing`. Foreign ownership fractions stored per company; the `foreign_acquisition` crisis fires from real holdings instead of a random draw.
- **Required capacity:** Listing requires an operating `commodity_exchange` (the venue where shares trade) and the company must have at least three assets and a clean quarter — an insolvent charter cannot float. The state must actually hold the equity it sells: float proceeds are one-time.
- **Interactions:** Deepens every existing charter system: `PERSONALITY_DEFS` weights now play out under shareholder pressure (a `profit_driven` board pays dividends; an `expansionist` one never does, and minority holders revolt). Espionage gains a new target class — falsified filings feed the `accounting_fraud` crisis. The press system moves share prices with scandal coverage.
- **Strategic advantages:** Float windfalls are the biggest single injections of credits available at Tier 1, and buying into a rival's key logistics charter is influence no diplomacy screen offers.
- **Vulnerabilities & consequences:** You are selling control. A foreign power holding 30% of your frontier colonization charter has a vote in its board crises and a veto on its loyalty — and the existing `hostile_takeover_bid` and `shareholder_revolt` crises become weapons pointed at you. Listed companies also resist wartime direction: nationalizing a listed charter (`CORP_NATIONALIZE`) now expropriates foreign shareholders, a diplomatic incident with every holder.
- **Emergent follow-ons:** Cross-holding webs that make two empires' corporate sectors inseparable before their governments ever sign a treaty; charter companies playing their state shareholders against foreign ones; the first "too foreign-owned to nationalize" crisis.

## Tier 2 — Specialization

### Algorithmic Markets (`econ_automated_markets`)
- **Domain:** economy | **Tier:** 2
- **Prerequisites:** econ_commodity_exchanges, comp_distributed_compute
- **Concept:** Once exchange order books are machine-readable, trading becomes a computation problem: standing algorithms that watch prices and act in milliseconds replace human traders who act in days. Liquidity improves enormously — until every algorithm tries to exit at once.
- **What becomes possible:** The faction's commercial apparatus trades continuously without orders from the palace. Market activity detaches from the player's attention.
- **Gameplay mechanic unlocked:** Standing trading policies. The player defines per-resource bands — accumulate below price X, liquidate above Y, maintain a reserve floor of Z — and the tick worker executes them against exchange prices every economy tick, no manual orders required. This converts the market from something you *visit* into infrastructure you *program*, and frees attention for war and politics. It also introduces the flash crash: when multiple factions' algorithms share a sell trigger, one bad tick cascades.
- **Engine hook:** New order `ECON_SET_TRADING_POLICY {resource, buyBelow, sellAbove, reserveFloor}` persisting an `algorithmicPolicies` map in `EconomyWorldState`; a policy-execution pass added to the economy tick just before `updateMarketsAggregated`, so algorithmic flow feeds the same supply/demand inputs as organic trade. Flash-crash detection: if aggregate algorithmic sells exceed a threshold fraction of `Market.supply` in one tick, `volatility` spikes and a press-system event is emitted. Unlock flag `algo_trading`.
- **Required capacity:** Requires live compute: a research-type planet (or dedicated compute district on the 64-district surface map) allocated as the trading engine's host, consuming `energyLoad` continuously. If that planet is blockaded or its power grid fails, your algorithms go dark mid-position — the policies suspend but your open exposure does not.
- **Interactions:** The prerequisite for `econ_market_warfare` (you cannot raid a market faster than humans react without machines) and `econ_futarchy`. Espionage op opportunity: stealing a rival's trading parameters (`ESP_*` op against the host planet) lets you trade exactly one band ahead of them, invisibly, until they change the policy.
- **Strategic advantages:** A programmed economy compounds while you sleep; in the polling-based client (`useGameSync`), this is the difference between an economy that needs babysitting and one that runs. Superior bands beat superior reserves.
- **Vulnerabilities & consequences:** You have automated your own vulnerability. Stolen parameters are undetectable exploitation; flash crashes destroy liquidity precisely when several players are stressed simultaneously; and the population reads "the machines sold our grain reserve during the famine" exactly as you'd expect — algorithmic sales of scarce essentials raise `instability` faster than manual ones, because there is no minister to blame.
- **Emergent follow-ons:** An arms race of band-tuning and parameter espionage; informal "circuit breaker" treaties after the first galactic flash crash; parliaments demanding oversight of the algorithms, setting up the Tier 4 futarchy question.

### Futures Markets (`econ_futures_markets`)
- **Domain:** economy | **Tier:** 2
- **Prerequisites:** econ_commodity_exchanges, comp_predictive_systems
- **Concept:** A futures contract sells certainty: a promise of delivery at a fixed price on a fixed date lets producers and consumers trade away risk — and lets speculators buy it. Pricing that risk at societal scale requires predictive modeling of harvests, wars, and shipping losses.
- **What becomes possible:** The civilization can hedge time itself — locking in tomorrow's prices for war materiel, harvests, and freight — and can underwrite the physical risks of the spacelanes.
- **Gameplay mechanic unlocked:** Two instruments. **Forwards:** binding contracts to deliver a resource volume at a strike price on a future tick — the war-planner's tool (lock in `ammo` and `metals` prices *before* declaring the war that will spike them) and the speculator's (buy grain futures on a rival you intend to blockade). **Underwriting:** insure a trade route against its `piracyRisk` and `blockadeRisk` for a premium priced off those very fields; losses on insured routes pay out from the underwriter's reserves. Both create counterparty risk as a live gameplay object: contracts can be defaulted on, and defaults are public.
- **Engine hook:** New `forwardContracts` collection in `EconomyWorldState` with `{resource, volume, strikePrice, deliveryTick, margin, parties}`; orders `ECON_OPEN_FORWARD`, `ECON_CLOSE_FORWARD`, `ECON_UNDERWRITE_ROUTE`. Margin is checked each economy tick against `Faction.liquidity` — margin calls force liquidation into the spot market (feeding `updateMarketsAggregated`, so a big margin call visibly moves prices). At `deliveryTick`, settlement instantiates a short-lived `TradeAgreement` with `priceFormula: 'fixed'`, reusing the existing delivery/routing machinery. Underwriting hooks the piracy-service loss events: an insured loss transfers credits from underwriter to holder.
- **Required capacity:** Contracts trade only on a `commodity_exchange` venue; underwriting requires a chartered corporation with the `banking` or `trade` mission to act as book-runner, and reserves — an underwriter whose treasury can't cover its written book is one pirate season from the existing `debt_crisis` / `bankruptcy` corporate crises. Physical settlement needs real logistics capacity on the delivery date: promising ore you cannot haul is default.
- **Interactions:** `comp_predictive_systems` quality determines how well the AI prices premiums; `esp_predictive_intel` lets you trade on stolen forecasts. War declarations instantly reprice open forwards — the existing `WarState` demand multipliers become tradeable events. Feeds TRADE and WEALTH season scoring.
- **Strategic advantages:** The only mechanism in the game for moving cost *through time*: fight this season's war at last season's prices. Underwriting turns other people's piracy problem into your income stream.
- **Vulnerabilities & consequences:** Leverage. Margin calls arrive exactly when you are least liquid, and a defaulted delivery craters `confidenceIndex` and future coupon rates. Underwriters invite moral hazard — a faction that insures its rival's routes has just paid that rival's pirates' wages if the rival colludes with them (insurance fraud: a genuinely new espionage-adjacent scam). Speculation losses are politically toxic: a treasury that gambles the grain budget answers to parliament.
- **Emergent follow-ons:** Cornering attempts on `rare` resources before megaproject construction; insurance syndicates that start paying for anti-piracy patrols out of self-interest; the first war postponed six weeks because the aggressor's forward book hadn't matured.

### Planetary Development Finance (`econ_dev_finance`)
- **Domain:** economy | **Tier:** 2
- **Prerequisites:** econ_interstellar_banking, soc_digital_governance
- **Concept:** Development finance converts a place's future output into present construction: bonds secured against a specific colony's tax base, repaid over decades. It requires both credit markets and an administration transparent enough that lenders believe the colony's books.
- **What becomes possible:** Planets stop being funded from the imperial treasury alone. Any world's development can be accelerated with anyone's money — including money from empires that do not wish that world well.
- **Gameplay mechanic unlocked:** Planet-backed development bonds. The owner floats a bond against a named planet; proceeds go into a construction escrow that boosts that planet's `derived.construction` and funds specified buildings/districts on its 64-district surface map, while coupons are paid from the planet's actual output stockpile. The strategic novelty: **foreign factions can buy these bonds.** Buying a rival's colony debt is simultaneously an investment, an intelligence feed (bondholders see the planet's production reports), and a leash — unpaid coupons accumulate `defaultPressure` that, with `econ_debt_leverage`, converts into concessions. You can develop your frontier at a stranger's expense, or fund a stranger's frontier and own its future.
- **Engine hook:** New order `ECON_ISSUE_DEV_BOND {planetId, amount, couponRate, term}` creating `DevelopmentBond` records in `EconomyWorldState`; escrow disbursement and coupon settlement run in the economy tick against the planet's `PlanetProduction.stockpile`; bondholder production-report access exposed through the sync snapshot. Unlock flag `dev_finance`. Charter corps with the `colonization` mission may issue these for their own colony seeds, with the state as guarantor — formalizing the implicit bargain in the existing `colony_seed` asset.
- **Required capacity:** The issuing planet needs a functioning administration — `soc_digital_governance` platforms deployed and service status no worse than `strained` — because lenders price against audited books. The escrow can only build as fast as local logistics allow (`PlanetLogisticsState` caps disbursement). A `central_reserve_bank` must exist to register the instruments.
- **Interactions:** The direct feeder for `econ_debt_leverage`. Interacts with secession mechanics: a colony whose development was foreign-financed carries its debt into independence — rebels inherit the coupon, and their creditors suddenly have opinions about the civil war. Development spending raises `happiness` and `serviceSatisfaction` on the target world, making this the economic counter to unrest.
- **Strategic advantages:** Growth without taxation and without waiting; the only way to project economic influence into territory you cannot reach militarily. As a lender, it converts idle treasury into both yield and leverage.
- **Vulnerabilities & consequences:** Mortgaged worlds are restless worlds: populations that watch their output ship out as coupon payments to a foreign bank accumulate `unrestRisk`, and the press system will write the "company planet" story for you. As a borrower, stacked dev bonds are a commitment trap — the existing `COMMITMENT_TRAP` effect type finally earns its name. As a lender, defaults in a war you didn't start torch your treasury.
- **Emergent follow-ons:** Debt-trap diplomacy as a play style; independence movements campaigning on repudiation; bond-holding blocs intervening in other people's rebellions to protect their paper.

### Directed Capital (`econ_state_capital`)
- **Domain:** economy | **Tier:** 2
- **Prerequisites:** econ_public_charters, econ_dev_finance
- **Concept:** The developmental-state model: banks and chartered companies as instruments of national strategy, credit allocated by ministry rather than market. It builds astonishing things on schedule, and misallocates capital invisibly until the bill arrives at once.
- **What becomes possible:** The state commands its corporate sector. Capital goes where policy points, not where returns beckon.
- **Gameplay mechanic unlocked:** Corporate direction. The state may order any domestic chartered corporation to execute a specific project — build a named asset in a named system, fund a megaproject tranche, open a route — at the company's expense, paying with loyalty rather than credits. Foreign ownership of domestic charters is capped by statute, and corporate `autonomyDrift` is structurally suppressed. The corporate sector becomes a second, obedient construction arm — and stops being a source of surprises, good ones included.
- **Engine hook:** `mutuallyExclusiveGroup: 'econ_t2_capital_doctrine'`. New order `CORP_DIRECT_INVESTMENT {companyId, directive}` handled in the game-loop `CORP_*` block, bypassing the personality-weighted growth engine for that action; global modifiers `corp_autonomy_drift_mult: 0.6` and `foreign_share_cap: 0.24` applied through the charter service; `DEMAND_DEFS` frequency reduced via `demandRate` multiplier. Directed projects skip the megaproject proposal flow — the state proposes, the company complies.
- **Required capacity:** Direction is administration: each standing directive occupies political-capital upkeep, and the ministry needs `soc_digital_governance` platforms to audit compliance. Companies with `corrupt` or `monopolist` personalities comply on paper and skim in practice — corruption drift rises under direction, and only functioning oversight (espionage's counterintel apparatus turned inward) catches it.
- **Interactions:** Synergizes with war economy: directed shipyard charters build naval capacity without market prices spiking. Anti-synergizes with `econ_automated_markets` (your exchanges thin out as the corporate sector stops trading opportunistically) and halves exchange fee income. Locks out `econ_free_port_compact`.
- **Strategic advantages:** Certainty. Wartime and megaproject execution without negotiation; no `foreign_acquisition` crises; the charter sector cannot be bought out from under you.
- **Vulnerabilities & consequences:** You inherit every misallocation: directed companies do not go profitably rogue, they go quietly bankrupt, and the state guarantee behind them means their `debt_crisis` is your treasury's crisis. Innovation-personality companies wither (`funded_research` weights effectively zeroed under direction), slowing your research economy. Foreign capital treats your market as closed and prices your bonds accordingly — coupon rates carry a permanent dirigisme premium.
- **Emergent follow-ons:** Five-year-plan megaproject sprints; a corporate sector that has forgotten how to act without orders collapsing when the state that directed it fractures; defection of your best boards to free-port rivals.

### Free Port Compact (`econ_free_port_compact`)
- **Domain:** economy | **Tier:** 2
- **Prerequisites:** econ_public_charters, econ_commodity_exchanges
- **Concept:** The entrepôt model: sovereignty funded by fees instead of taxes. The state guarantees contracts, keeps the ports open to all flags, and takes basis points on everything that moves — governing least so that the most passes through.
- **What becomes possible:** The civilization becomes the galaxy's counter and clearinghouse: anyone's corporations may operate in its space, and its income is the traffic itself.
- **Gameplay mechanic unlocked:** Open registry. Foreign chartered corporations may establish assets in your territory under your host policy (extending the existing `CORP_SET_HOST_POLICY` order into a standing invitation), paying registry and handling fees per asset per tick. Tariffs are constitutionally capped near zero, replaced by fee income that scales with total resident corporate activity — yours *and* everyone else's. Charter founding costs halve and territory grants widen, but corporate `autonomyDrift` runs structurally hot: the compact cuts both ways.
- **Engine hook:** `mutuallyExclusiveGroup: 'econ_t2_capital_doctrine'`. Unlock flag `free_port`; `PolicyState.tariffsByResource` hard-capped at 0.05 by a policy validator in the game loop; per-asset registry fee stream added to the corporate revenue pass; `TERRITORY_DEFS.charterCost` and `RIGHT_DEFS.charterCost` halved via a global modifier; `corp_autonomy_drift_mult: 1.4`. Foreign corps resident in your space are listed in the sync snapshot — visible to your rivals' owners, too.
- **Required capacity:** A free port with no port is a slogan: fee income requires at least one `commodity_exchange` venue and `TradeHub` status in the registry systems, plus naval protection of the approaches — traffic goes where `piracyRisk` is low, and the compact obliges you to protect foreign corporate assets you do not control.
- **Interactions:** Compounds with `econ_galactic_clearing` into the full mercantile-hegemon build. Resident foreign corps are both income and hostages-in-reverse: their home states acquire an interest in your survival (attacking the free port means shooting their own balance sheets). Espionage delights in open registries — every foreign warehouse is potential cover for an agent network, yours or theirs.
- **Strategic advantages:** The highest credit ceiling in the game when trade volume is high; wars between other powers *raise* your revenue as trade reroutes through neutral ports; soft power without a fleet to match.
- **Vulnerabilities & consequences:** Your prosperity is a derivative of other people's peace — a galaxy-wide war collapses fee income overnight with no tax base to fall back on. Autonomy-hot charters generate demands (`greater_autonomy`, `customs_authority`) constantly, and the compact makes refusing them costly. You cannot close the registry selectively without breaking the promise the whole model rests on: expelling one power's corps reprices every other tenant's trust in you. Locks out `econ_state_capital`.
- **Emergent follow-ons:** The neutral-entrepôt survival strategy for small factions; smuggling and sanctions-evasion economies routing through your ports whether you like it or not; the day a resident foreign corporation's private fleet outguns your navy in your own harbor.

## Tier 3 — Dominance

### Reserve Currency Clearing (`econ_galactic_clearing`)
- **Domain:** economy | **Tier:** 3
- **Prerequisites:** econ_interstellar_banking, econ_automated_markets, comm_quantum_secure
- **Concept:** Whoever operates the settlement layer of interstellar trade collects a toll on everything and can excommunicate anyone. Reserve status is not declared — it accretes to whichever clearing system is most trusted, and quantum-secure channels are what make that trust unforgeable.
- **What becomes possible:** The faction's clearing network becomes the medium through which other empires settle *their* trade with each other. Their commerce runs on your rails.
- **Gameplay mechanic unlocked:** The clearing network. Factions may join (voluntarily — that's the point) to gain a trade-efficiency bonus on all routes settled through it; the operator collects basis points on every member's `throughputPerHour`, including trade between two *other* members. The weapon this creates: financial sanction. The operator can suspend any member, instantly stripping their efficiency bonus, freezing their in-network balances, and marking their instruments unclearable — a blockade with no fleet. The dilemma this creates: every use of the weapon teaches every member why they need an alternative.
- **Engine hook:** New orbital structure `clearing_nexus`; `clearingNetwork: {operatorId, members, feeBps, suspended}` in `EconomyWorldState`; per-tick fee sweep computed from existing `TradeHub.throughputPerHour`; new order `ECON_CLEARING_SANCTION {factionId}` — distinct from and compounding with the existing `PolicyState.sanctions`. A permanent `defection` counter: each sanction event increments every member's incentive to found a rival network (AI factions with `econ_interstellar_banking` may then build competing nexuses, permanently splitting the fee pool).
- **Required capacity:** The nexus needs a quantum-secure relay backbone (`comm_quantum_secure` infrastructure physically deployed on relay worlds) and a `central_reserve_bank` with reserves proportional to member volume — a clearing house that cannot make members whole after a flash crash stops being one. Member trust is capacity: `confidenceIndex` below threshold suspends *your* operator status story in the press.
- **Interactions:** With `econ_free_port_compact`, this is the complete mercantile hegemon: the galaxy trades in your ports and settles on your books. Season scoring: dominant clearing share should feed the TRADE and INFLUENCE categories. Espionage against the nexus (`esp_sigint` lineage) reads member settlement flows — the single richest intelligence target in the game, which is exactly why members demanded quantum security.
- **Strategic advantages:** Structural income from other people's economies; the only sanction mechanism that works without adjacency or a navy; visibility into galactic trade flows as an operator privilege.
- **Vulnerabilities & consequences:** The exorbitant privilege invites the exorbitant backlash: over-use of `ECON_CLEARING_SANCTION` triggers coordinated de-dollarization, and a successful rival network halves your fee base forever — the weapon depletes itself. The nexus is a strategic target: its destruction (or capture in a civil war) freezes settled trade galaxy-wide, making *you* systemically important in the way that gets megastructures shot at. Operating it honestly constrains you: freezing a member's balances to fund your own war is the one move that ends the network overnight.
- **Emergent follow-ons:** Bipolar clearing blocs as the economic shape of cold war; small factions keeping balances in both networks as neutrality insurance; the operator bailing out a hostile member during a crash purely to preserve the network — mercy as infrastructure maintenance.

### Sovereign Leverage (`econ_debt_leverage`)
- **Domain:** economy | **Tier:** 3
- **Prerequisites:** econ_dev_finance, esp_predictive_intel
- **Concept:** Debt becomes power at the moment of refinancing: a creditor who knows precisely when a debtor cannot pay chooses that moment to ask for something other than money. Predictive intelligence fusion is what turns a loan book into a foreign-policy instrument.
- **What becomes possible:** The lending relationships built at Tiers 1–2 mature into a full coercive repertoire: call the loan, or name the concession.
- **Gameplay mechanic unlocked:** Loan enforcement. Against any debtor in arrears (faction bonds, dev-bond coupons, or guaranteed corporate paper), the creditor may issue a call: the debtor must repay principal immediately, or choose from a concession menu — pledged collateral transfers (orbital structures, corporate assets, exchange fee shares), charter rights for your corporations inside their territory, tariff exemptions, or a public austerity program (their `PolicyState` constrained, their stability bleeding, your paper serviced first). Collateral pledged at origination becomes seizable title, and seizures hand real assets to your chartered corps — the East India Company loop, closed.
- **Engine hook:** New orders `ECON_CALL_LOAN {contractId}` and `ECON_ACCEPT_CONCESSION {option}`; `LoanContract` gains `collateral` and `arrearsTicks` fields; austerity implemented as a timed `PolicyState` constraint plus per-tick stability drain in the debtor's government model. Seized corporate assets transfer via the existing charter asset ledger. Every enforcement emits press-system coverage in both empires — the debtor's public reads it as humiliation, which is the mechanic, not flavor.
- **Required capacity:** Enforcement requires standing to enforce: either a clearing network the debtor belongs to (`econ_galactic_clearing` — freeze their settlements until they choose), or physical reach (fleet presence or corporate assets in their territory to actually take possession). A creditor with neither gets repudiation, and repudiation is the debtor's counter-move: eat the confidence collapse, keep the assets.
- **Interactions:** `esp_predictive_intel` shows you debtor liquidity *before* you call — calling a loan the debtor can pay is a wasted incident. Chains with secession mechanics: offering a rebelling colony assumption of its dev-bond debt in exchange for alignment is now a play. Austerity-driven instability feeds the existing rebellion pressure systems — you can, with patience, foreclose on a civil war.
- **Strategic advantages:** Conquest without occupation: concessions extract value that raiding never could, and every concession compounds (charter rights beget assets beget influence). Against AI factions it converts patient lending into territory-adjacent power.
- **Vulnerabilities & consequences:** Debt-trap statecraft is visible in aggregate: factions watching your enforcement pattern stop borrowing from you, shrinking the very loan book your power rests on — predation exhausts its prey. Repudiation cascades are systemic risk: one high-profile default emboldens others, and your balance sheet holds all of it. Enforced austerity manufactures revanchism; the debtor's next government runs on burning your paper, and the press system will make your banks the villain of a generation.
- **Emergent follow-ons:** Anti-creditor coalitions and jubilee diplomacy (rivals buying out debtors to flip them); corporate creditor states where the loan book, not parliament, sets foreign policy; the debtor who defaults *strategically* to bait an enforcement they can cast as casus belli.

### Coordinated Market Warfare (`econ_market_warfare`)
- **Domain:** economy | **Tier:** 3
- **Prerequisites:** econ_futures_markets, econ_automated_markets, comm_infowar_suite
- **Concept:** A market is a consensus about the future, and consensus can be attacked: coordinated short positions, engineered liquidity droughts, and synchronized narrative injection can crash real economies without touching real goods. The information-warfare layer is what makes the panic self-sustaining.
- **What becomes possible:** Economic collapse as a targetable, deniable weapon system — the offensive doctrine of the mercantile civilization.
- **Gameplay mechanic unlocked:** The market raid. Against a target theatre and resource, the attacker commits liquidity to a coordinated operation: shorts opened through the futures book, algorithmic sell pressure through the exchange, and planted press coverage through the infowar apparatus, synchronized over a chosen tick window. A successful raid spikes volatility, craters the target's `confidenceIndex`, and pumps `collapsePressure` into the target's `EconomicRegion` — pushing toward the existing collapse-stage machinery (`strained` → `critical` → `collapsing`) and, at the limit, the `CollapseEvent` regime outcomes. Raids are gradual-burn operations with an attribution risk per tick, espionage-style: discovered raids convert economic damage into diplomatic catastrophe.
- **Engine hook:** New order `ECON_MARKET_RAID {targetTheatreId, resource, commitment, durationTicks}` following the `ESP_*` operation pattern (queued, resolved over ticks, with detection rolls against the target's counterintelligence and market-surveillance capacity); effects applied as distortions to the supply/demand inputs of `updateMarketsAggregated` plus direct `collapsePressure` injection; press integration plants stories through the existing propagation system rather than a parallel channel, so the target's own press-reading tools can catch the pattern. Unlock flag `market_warfare`.
- **Required capacity:** Raids burn real liquidity (the commitment is spent, win or lose) and require all three arms deployed: an algorithmic trading engine, open access to a venue that lists the target's markets (a free-port or shared exchange — closed economies are largely immune, which is *their* compensation), and infowar infrastructure in relay range. Covertly acquired stakes in the target's listed charters (`CORP_BUY_SHARES` through intermediaries) multiply raid effect — another reason `econ_public_charters` cuts both ways.
- **Interactions:** Devastating against `econ_free_port_compact` factions (open, listed, exposed) and nearly useless against `econ_state_capital` ones — the Tier 2 fork becomes a defensive posture choice retroactively. Counterplay lives in this same tree: exchange hosts can halt trading (fee loss vs contagion), clearing operators can backstop confidence, underwriters absorb the first losses. Feeds DISRUPTION season scoring.
- **Strategic advantages:** Wars won before declaration: a rival entering a shooting war mid-collapse fights at a permanent materiel deficit. Deniability separates it from blockade — the only theatre-scale attack that doesn't move a fleet.
- **Vulnerabilities & consequences:** Contagion does not check flags: crashing a theatre your own algorithms and underwriters trade in burns your own book, and clearing-network membership transmits the shock back up your rails. Attribution is ruinous — a discovered raid unites the room against you and hands the target a legitimacy windfall. Repeated raids teach the galaxy to close its markets, strangling the open-trade world your whole civilization identity feeds on: the arsonist's problem, in finance.
- **Emergent follow-ons:** Market-surveillance institutions as the new counterintelligence; raid-and-invade combined arms doctrine; the false-flag raid, engineered to be "discovered" as someone else's.

## Tier 4 — Transformation

### Stellar Securitization (`econ_stellar_securities`)
- **Domain:** economy | **Tier:** 4
- **Prerequisites:** econ_galactic_clearing, econ_futures_markets, mega_orbital_habitats
- **Concept:** No treasury can pay for a Dyson swarm; a market can. Securitizing a megastructure means selling tradeable claims on its future output before it exists — pricing a structure that will outlive every government that regulates it, and every buyer.
- **What becomes possible:** Megastructures become financial objects. Star-scale industry is built on other people's capital, and ownership of civilization's largest works fragments across the galaxy.
- **Gameplay mechanic unlocked:** Output shares. The owner of a megastructure project (orbital ring, Dyson swarm tranche, orbital elevator, or the corporate megaprojects in `MEGAPROJECT_DEFS`) may issue perpetual shares in its future output — energy stream, throughput fees, or credit income — sold on exchanges to any faction or chartered corporation. Proceeds flow into the project's construction escrow, compressing build time by seasons. Shares trade forever after: a Dyson swarm's output becomes the galaxy's deepest instrument, and its price becomes the reference asset the entire financial system marks against. The decision this forces: how much of your civilization's permanent energy base are you willing to have owned abroad, in exchange for having it at all?
- **Engine hook:** New order `ECON_SECURITIZE {projectId, outputFraction, tranches}`; `StellarShare` instruments in `EconomyWorldState` with per-tick output distribution wired to the megastructure's production entry (for `mega_dyson_swarm`, the energy stream itself is divisible — shareholders receive `energy` credits at the swarm's realized output); construction escrow accelerates the existing megaproject `durationDays` clock in proportion to funded tranches. Unlock flag `stellar_securities`. Buyback via standard exchange orders at market price — there is no cheap exit.
- **Required capacity:** Requires an actual eligible project in progress (megastructure capability from the `mega_*` line — knowledge without a swarm to sell is a prospectus), a clearing network to settle perpetual distributions, and demonstrated orbital-habitat-scale construction (`mega_orbital_habitats`) for the market to believe delivery. Distribution integrity is capacity: missed output payments (structure damaged, blockaded, or overpromised) are defaults with `confidenceIndex` consequences at reserve-currency scale.
- **Interactions:** The financing key that unlocks the practical construction of `mega_orbital_ring` and `mega_dyson_swarm` for factions without hegemonic treasuries — a mid-power with excellent credit can out-build a superpower with bad paper. Foreign shareholders in your swarm acquire a hard interest in your territorial integrity (their asset orbits your star): securitization is inadvertent alliance engineering. Charter corps can hold and trade stellar shares, creating corporations wealthier than states.
- **Strategic advantages:** Compresses the endgame build race decisively; converts a megastructure from a cost center into the anchor asset of a financial order you operate; entangles rivals' wealth with your survival.
- **Vulnerabilities & consequences:** You have sold pieces of your star. Full sovereignty over your own energy base is gone at any price you'd pay; wartime energy rationing that shorts shareholders is simultaneous default against every holder, including neutral ones. The reference-asset role transmits your local disasters globally — damage to the swarm is a galactic financial event, making it the ultimate strategic target and *you* the ultimate systemic risk. And a structure that outlives governments raises the succession question: when your empire fractures, the civil war's first battle is over who services the shares.
- **Emergent follow-ons:** Consortium megastructures owned by no single empire; charter corporations whose stellar-share portfolios make them great powers without territory; the heirloom problem — century-old output shares held by factions that no longer exist, and the courts, or fleets, that decide what they're worth now.

### The Futarchic Mandate (`econ_futarchy`)
- **Domain:** economy | **Tier:** 4
- **Prerequisites:** econ_futures_markets, econ_automated_markets, comp_autonomous_administration
- **Concept:** Futarchy: vote on values, bet on beliefs. Citizens and institutions define what outcomes the civilization wants; conditional prediction markets — not ministers, not parliaments — decide which policies get enacted, by pricing which one the traders collectively believe will deliver. The market stops advising the sovereign and becomes the sovereign.
- **What becomes possible:** The corporate-mercantile civilization completes its logic: governance itself is executed by the price mechanism, administered by machine, and no one is exactly in charge — which is presented, sincerely, as the feature.
- **Gameplay mechanic unlocked:** Policy domains ceded to decision markets. The player designates governance areas — trade policy, development budgets, even military appropriations — as *mandated*: within them, `PolicyState` changes are proposed freely (by the player, parliament factions, charter corps, even AI institutions), and conditional markets resolve which proposal is enacted, scored against declared outcome metrics drawn from the season-scoring categories (WEALTH, STABILITY, TRADE...). Mandated domains cost zero political capital to operate and outperform manual play statistically — the machine-market genuinely governs well — but the player is voluntarily surrendering levers, and parliament knows exactly what it is being replaced by. Coalition politics reorganizes around the one question left: which domains remain human.
- **Engine hook:** `mutuallyExclusiveGroup: 'econ_t4_market_endstate'`. New government mode fields `futarchyDomains: string[]` on the government state; a decision-market resolution pass in the game loop (reusing the forward-contract machinery with policy outcomes as the underlying) replaces parliament votes for mandated domains; political-capital costs zeroed there, while parliament cohesion mechanics reprice — each domain ceded permanently transfers weight from the chamber to the exchange. Charter corps trade in decision markets by right: `senate_representation` demands are superseded by something better than a seat.
- **Required capacity:** Requires the full stack running live: algorithmic trading engine, autonomous administration (`comp_autonomous_administration`) to execute resolved policy without human ministries, deep liquid markets (thin decision markets are manipulable decision markets), and a literate participating population — worlds with `failing` education-class services produce markets that price superstition. Compute blockade or flash crash suspends governance itself in mandated domains: the state, briefly, has no way to decide things.
- **Interactions:** The endpoint `econ_market_warfare` was warning about: a rival who can raid your markets can now, at the margin, *vote in your government* — decision-market manipulation becomes the supreme espionage target and the supreme espionage prize. Press-system narratives move policy directly, since traders price on them. With `econ_free_port_compact` heritage this is apotheosis; a `econ_state_capital` civilization reaching for it must first unwind its own doctrine, which parliament may read as a coup by spreadsheet.
- **Strategic advantages:** Structurally superior policy throughput at zero political capital; immunity to normal coalition gridlock; the ideological high ground of demonstrable results — legitimacy from performance rather than consent.
- **Vulnerabilities & consequences:** Legitimacy from performance is legitimacy that dies with the first crash: one flash-crash-induced policy absurdity (the markets briefly resolving to defund the fleet during an invasion) fuels secessionists, traditionalist coups, and every "restore human rule" movement at once — and the coup/civil-war machinery should price futarchy resentment explicitly. Manipulation is existential rather than expensive. And the mandate is a commitment trap in the deepest sense: domains ceded are politically almost impossible to reclaim, because the institution you'd need to vote them back has already been hollowed out. Locks out `econ_commons_transition`.
- **Emergent follow-ons:** Civilizations governed well by something no one can name a leader of; opposition movements that must *out-trade* the government to change it; the first war between a futarchy and a commons — fought, on one side, because the markets priced it as positive expected value.

### The Great Decommodification (`econ_commons_transition`)
- **Domain:** economy | **Tier:** 4
- **Prerequisites:** econ_dev_finance, mat_molecular_manufacturing, phys_fusion_power
- **Concept:** When molecular manufacturing and fusion abundance make marginal production cost collapse toward zero, price stops carrying information about scarcity — because there isn't any. A civilization can then choose to stop pricing survival: essentials become commons, and the market retreats to the edges of life instead of constituting it.
- **What becomes possible:** The other answer to this domain's final question. The mercantile civilization uses its mastery of markets one last time — to retire them from everything that matters.
- **Gameplay mechanic unlocked:** The commons transition, a staged and irreversible reconstitution. Essentials (`food`, `energy`, housing-equivalent services) are decommodified on all owned worlds: `localPrices` for those resources pin to zero, `commodityScarcity` and `essentialShortage` mechanics are permanently disarmed where fabricator capacity suffices, and `happiness`/`serviceSatisfaction` re-baseline high. Internal credits demonetize; external trade continues through a single state trading window (a monopsony interface to the galactic market — you still buy `rare` and sell surplus, but as one actor). Every domestic chartered corporation faces the founder's choice writ final: convert to a public trust (assets retained, profit motive and influence mechanics retired) or emigrate to a foreign registry, taking its fleet and its grudge with it. The player trades the entire economic weapons suite of Tiers 1–3 for a society that cannot be starved, bought, or crashed.
- **Engine hook:** `mutuallyExclusiveGroup: 'econ_t4_market_endstate'`. Staged transition orders `ECON_COMMONS_STAGE {stage}` (announce → essentials → demonetize → corporate resolution), each an irreversible flag on faction state; per-planet eligibility check against fabricator/energy capacity each economy tick; `CORP_CONVERT_TRUST` and forced-emigration resolution added to the charter service; the state trading window implemented as a synthetic single-actor participant in the existing market and exchange systems. WEALTH season scoring is retired for this faction and replaced by scoring weight shifted to STABILITY and POPULATION — the score screen itself should record that this civilization now keeps different books.
- **Required capacity:** Honesty matters most here: the transition is only as real as the fabricators. Each world needs deployed molecular-manufacturing capacity and fusion energy surplus covering its population's essential demand — worlds below threshold stay priced until built out, and a blockade that cuts fabricator feedstock (`rare` inputs still come from somewhere) can re-impose scarcity on a population that has forgotten how to queue. Post-scarcity is a supply chain, not a decree.
- **Interactions:** Immunizes against the entire economic warfare stack — market raids find no market, debt leverage finds no borrower, clearing sanctions find one state window that can route around them. Foreign creditors holding your pre-transition paper must be settled or repudiated at the demonetization stage: the transition's price includes every old debt at once. Emigrated corporations become permanent foreign actors with intimate knowledge of your infrastructure — some will come back as somebody's espionage cover.
- **Strategic advantages:** Unmatched stability and legitimacy: near-immunity to unrest-through-scarcity, rebellion pressure, and economic coercion; a population-and-happiness engine no market civilization can match; the moral-example effect on the galaxy's restive colonies is a soft-power weapon you never have to aim.
- **Vulnerabilities & consequences:** You have disarmed yourself of everything sharp in this tree: no leverage, no raids, no fee income, no float windfalls — external power projection must come from other domains entirely, and allies who relied on your capital feel abandoned. The single trading window is a single point of failure and a sanctions magnet. Internally, the transition is the most dangerous political act in the game: every charter corporation, creditor, and market-adjacent elite is expropriated or exiled in one generation, and the corporate emigration wave arms your rivals with your own best institutions — expect the secession and coup machinery to run hot *during* the stages, when the old economy is dismantled and the commons not yet load-bearing. Locks out `econ_futarchy`.
- **Emergent follow-ons:** Commons federations absorbing scarcity-worn colonies that no fleet could conquer; exile corporate diasporas funding restorationist movements for decades; the galaxy's strangest cold war — a futarchy and a commons, each existence-proof that the other's sacrifice was unnecessary.
